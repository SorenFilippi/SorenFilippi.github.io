Idle speed control of a 2.5 L five-cylinder SI engine

Context
ETH Zürich, Engine Systems
Team
4 students
My role
Lead · controller tuning, model integration
Tools
MATLAB, Simulink, test bench

Modern engines idle at around 700 rpm to save fuel, so a sudden load, such as the alternator or the AC compressor, can stall them. We designed a model-based idle speed controller and ran it on the real engine at ETH's test bench.

Model

A mean-value engine model in Simulink: throttle flow, intake manifold filling and emptying, volumetric efficiency, and Willans-type torque generation with the induction-to-power-stroke and ignition delays. Static maps were identified by linear least squares on quasi-static data. Manifold volume, efficiency, friction and inertia were fitted by nonlinear optimisation against dynamic throttle-step measurements, then validated on held-out runs.

Controller

Linearised around idle, with both delays replaced by 4th-order Padé elements, giving a 10-state plant. On top of it, an LQG controller with integral action on the throttle channel. The design uses two actuators on two time scales. Ignition retard changes torque within one engine segment, so it absorbs a disturbance immediately. The throttle acts through the manifold over several cycles, so it takes over the steady load while the ignition phases back to nominal. Targets were a minimum return difference of 0.5 and a crossover below 4 rad/s to cope with sensor noise, with Tustin discretisation at 1 ms.

How I tuned it

An LQGI controller has four knobs here: the integrator gain, the two input weights, and the observer weight. Instead of tuning them by hand, I set it up as a constrained optimisation. Each candidate controller was simulated on the nonlinear engine model against the reference and load profile recorded at the test bench, and scored with the competition cost. The constraints kept the design robust: return difference between 0.5 and 0.8, crossover below 4 rad/s, and observer poles about three times faster than the closed loop. A random search found feasible starting points, and fmincon did the rest. After the first bench session I re-ran it on the new recordings.

I also swept the nominal ignition retard from −10 to −60 °CA. More retard gives a bigger torque reserve to fight load steps, but costs combustion efficiency, which the cost function penalises. The design settled around −15 °CA.

Result on the real engine

< 0.5 rad/smean steady-state speed error
2.4 sback within 3 rad/s after a 1 kW load step at 157 rad/s
81 rad/slowest speed in the run; stall limit is 50 rad/s

Our first attempt on the bench held a steady speed offset and drove the ignition into its limit. The trace above is the final controller we handed in, and it shows the intended hand-off. When the load switches on, the ignition reacts first, then the throttle opens and takes over while the ignition phases back to its nominal value.

HydroArt: hybrid hydrogen-electric propulsion at 1/8 scale

Context
ISAE-SUPAERO student association
Team
Aerodynamics & propulsion
My role
Fuel cell sub-team
Hardware
Intelligent Energy IE-SOAR S800

HydroArt is building a 1/8 scale replica of a real aircraft, powered by a hybrid of a hydrogen fuel cell and a battery. I'm on the aerodynamics and propulsion team, in the fuel cell group. The cell covers cruise at a steady 800 W, and the battery supplies the extra power for take-off and climb, up to 4.8 kW.

A quick check from the datasheet: 50 g/h of hydrogen at 33.3 Wh/g (lower heating value) is about 1.7 kW of chemical power, for 800 W out. So the cell turns roughly half the hydrogen's energy into electricity, and the rest leaves as heat through those two fans. Our test campaign over the coming months will measure how the real stack compares.

How reliable is SWOT over small inland lakes?

Context
ETH Zürich, Space Geodesy research project
Team
6 students
My role
Initial strategy · data analysis
Tools
Python, PROJ

NASA/CNES's SWOT satellite measures water surface elevation with a wide-swath radar interferometer, which opens up lakes too small for classic nadir altimetry. We built a lake-independent Python pipeline that turns the SWOT L2 LakeSP product into a water level time series for any lake, then validated it against daily federal gauge records on five Swiss lakes.

The hard part was making two datasets comparable. Gauge heights are referenced to the Swiss LN02 datum and SWOT to the EGM2008 geoid, so we computed the geoid difference at each station. SWOT data also needed filtering on quality flags, partial lake coverage, physically plausible ranges and rates of change, and measurement uncertainty.

Finite element solvers, written from scratch

Context
ETH Zürich, Finite Element Analysis
Team
Individual
Scope
5 labs: bars, trusses, frames, CST, Q4
Tools
Python, NumPy, SciPy, gmsh

Five coding labs in which every part of the method is written by hand: element stiffness matrices, assembly, boundary conditions and post-processing, for explicit and implicit finite differences, trusses, 2D frames with Hermitian beam elements, constant-strain triangles and four-node quads with Gauss–Legendre quadrature. Three results stand out.

Stress concentration around a hole

A plate with a circular hole under uniaxial tension, meshed with constant-strain triangles. Theory (Kirsch) says the hoop stress at the edge of the hole peaks at exactly three times the remote stress. The FE solution reproduces the full stress distribution around the hole, and the error halves every time the element size halves: first-order convergence, which is what constant-strain elements should give.

Shear locking in Q4 elements

The same beam modelled with four-node quads, made thinner and thinner on a fixed mesh. A bilinear element cannot bend without also shearing, and with full 2×2 integration that spurious shear energy takes over: at a slenderness of 400 the beam deflects 500 times less than it should. Integrating the shear term at a single point removes the locking, and the solution stays on beam theory over the whole range. At the other end, for a beam as deep as it is long, the FE model deflects 3.5 times more than Euler–Bernoulli theory, because beam theory ignores shear deformation.

A 9,000-DOF frame

The frame solver, with static condensation and a sparse solver, run on a mystery input file of 8,563 beam elements clamped along one edge. It turned out to be the logo of the lab that teaches the course.

Image inpainting with a U-Net

ETH Zürich, Intro to ML · PyTorch

Reconstructing a hidden 8×8 patch at the centre of 28×28 handwritten digits. I built a three-level U-Net trained from scratch, with a loss weighted towards the masked patch since that is the only region scored, and cosine learning rate decay.

On 6,000 held-out digits the patch MSE is 0.053, against 0.185 for filling the hole with the average patch, so about 3.5 times lower. The network learns to continue strokes through the hole: it closes loops and joins lines.
